A fake media placement in China press release distribution is the sale of supposed media coverage that does not match what is actually delivered — fabricated screenshots instead of live URLs, lookalike domains carrying a famous media brand's name, or long media lists padded with pages that no editor ever reviewed. The scam is common precisely because most overseas buyers cannot easily verify a Chinese-language page on an unfamiliar domain.

This article explains how these schemes actually work, what evidence exposes each pattern, and the pre-payment checks that filter them out. It is written from the verification side of the market — the same principles behind our [media placement evaluation criteria](/how-we-evaluate-chinese-media-placements/) — so the checks here can be applied to any provider, including us.

## Why this scam targets overseas buyers specifically

Media list fraud is not unique to China, but three conditions make foreign buyers the preferred target:

1. **Language gap.** The buyer often cannot read the delivered page well enough to notice it says something different from the approved release.
2. **Domain unfamiliarity.** Most buyers do not know what an authentic Chinese media domain looks like, so a lookalike domain passes inspection.
3. **Payment timing.** The common model is full prepayment, so all leverage is with the seller once money has moved.

The result is a market where the seller controls the evidence, the buyer controls almost nothing — unless the buyer demands the right evidence before paying.

## The five fraud patterns

### Pattern 1: The screenshot report

The provider delivers a PDF or image report showing logos and page thumbnails, but no URLs — or URLs that do not open.

**Exposes it:** A real placement has a live, stable, publicly accessible URL. Screenshots are not evidence of publication; they are evidence that *someone once had something that looked like a page*. Refuse any report that does not come with clickable links, and open every link yourself from your own environment, not through the provider.

### Pattern 2: The lookalike domain

The delivered page genuinely exists, but the domain is not the media organization's real domain. It might be a copycat site, an expired-domain revival, or a private site using the media brand's name in its title and header.

**Exposes it:** Domain checking. Every established Chinese media organization publishes on known official domains. Compare the delivered domain against the outlet's official site — check the [China media directory](/china-media-directory/) approach of verifying against primary sources, or look up the domain's ICP registration (备案) information, which all legitimate Chinese websites must have. A news brand page on a domain with no connection to the actual organization is not a placement on that brand.

### Pattern 3: The vanishing placement

The URL is real at delivery and disappears days or weeks later — deleted by the platform, taken down for policy reasons, or removed by design because it was never meant to last.

**Exposes it:** Permanence terms plus archiving. Before payment, ask in writing how long placements remain live and what happens if one is removed. At delivery, archive the URL yourself (for example, save a dated snapshot or PDF of the page). A provider confident in its placements will have no problem with the buyer keeping evidence.

### Pattern 4: The padded media list

The media list contains 80 or 200 "outlets," but a large share are free self-publishing platforms, content accounts, aggregators and syndicated pages priced as if they were editorial placements on established media.

**Exposes it:** Line-item classification. Ask for the list broken down by page type: main-site news articles, syndicated pages, content accounts, self-publishing platforms. This is the same distinction that separates a meaningful [placement report](/china-media-guide/what-a-china-media-coverage-report-should-actually-show/) from a padded one. A list that resists being broken down is telling you what it is.

### Pattern 5: The phantom outlet

The media outlet on the list does not exist at all, or exists only as a name attached to a generic publishing page.

**Exposes it:** Existence checking. Search the outlet's name independently. A real Chinese media organization has an official website, an ICP record, and a footprint beyond the provider's own rate card. If the only place the outlet "exists" is inside the seller's price list, it is not an outlet.

## What all five patterns have in common

Every one of these schemes collapses under the same three demands:

1. **The actual URL, before or at delivery — opened by you, not shown by them.**
2. **The real domain, checked against the media organization's official presence.**
3. **Evidence that survives** — an archive or dated record of the page.

This is why our [placement principles](/our-media-placement-principles/) put verified publishing URLs and page-type classification at the center of delivery, rather than media counts. A provider that already operates this way will welcome these checks; one that resists them has answered your question before you asked it.

## A pre-payment checklist

Before paying any China press release provider:

* [ ] Get the specific outlets — named, with their real domains
* [ ] Ask what page type each placement will be (main-site article, syndicated page, content account)
* [ ] Confirm in writing that you receive live publishing URLs
* [ ] Agree how long placements remain live and what happens on removal
* [ ] Confirm the report distinguishes placement types instead of counting headlines
* [ ] Keep dated evidence of every delivered URL

None of these checks require Chinese-language ability or special access. They require only refusing to accept evidence the provider produced, in place of evidence you can verify yourself.

## The honest caveat

Not every bad outcome is fraud. China's publishing environment genuinely contains a wide spectrum of page types, editorial standards and placement durability, and a low-quality legitimate placement can look similar to a dishonest one from the outside. The difference is how the provider behaves when asked to classify and verify: a legitimate provider discloses page types and delivers checkable URLs even when the placements are modest, while a fraudulent one needs the ambiguity to survive.

That distinction — modest but honest versus impressive but unverifiable — is exactly what [independent verification](/verify-chinese-media-placement/) is designed to reveal, and it is the single question worth asking any provider before money changes hands.
