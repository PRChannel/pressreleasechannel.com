A media placement can look impressive on a spreadsheet and still be a poor fit for the campaign. The opposite can also happen.

A smaller industry publication may be more useful than a famous media brand if it reaches the right audience and presents the information in the right context.

## 1. Relevance comes before prestige

The first question should be: Is this media outlet relevant to the announcement?

Relevance can be based on industry, geography, business audience, consumer audience, announcement type and subject matter.

A famous media brand that has little connection to the topic may add less practical value than a smaller specialist publication.

## 2. Check the actual publishing domain

Do not evaluate a placement from the media name alone. Look at the URL.

Ask what domain hosts the page, whether it is the media's main website, a subdomain, a content platform or another publishing environment connected to the brand.

The actual domain tells you more about what was purchased.

## 3. Identify the page type

Two pages under the same brand can have very different meanings.

The placement may be a standard news article, industry channel page, regional page, content account, syndicated release, mobile page or another publisher-hosted format.

The buyer should know which one it is.

## 4. Confirm public accessibility

Can an ordinary user open the page?

Some pages may require an app, login, geographic access, a specific device or additional navigation.

For an overseas team, a publicly accessible URL is usually easier to verify and share internally.

## 5. Look for editorial signals

Useful signals can include a publication date, visible media section, byline or source line, consistent site navigation, standard article formatting and a clear relationship to the publication.

These signals do not guarantee audience size, but they help the buyer understand the page.

## 6. Ask whether the placement can be found later

If discoverability matters, ask whether the page can be found through site search, whether it is indexed by major search engines, and whether it is linked from any category page.

Do not assume the answer will be yes. Publication and discoverability are separate issues.

## 7. Consider durability

No media page should be assumed to remain online forever. But there is still value in asking whether the media usually retains older content, whether a support period exists and what happens if the page disappears shortly after publication.

## 8. Evaluate against the campaign objective

The same page can be useful for one campaign and weak for another.

If the objective is public information availability, a stable and verifiable page may be valuable. If the objective is journalist engagement, syndication alone may not be enough. If the objective is local credibility, regional and industry relevance may matter more than national brand recognition.

## 9. Be careful with screenshots as the main proof

Screenshots can document how a page looked at a specific moment, but they do not always show the real URL, whether the page is still live, or whether the page is publicly accessible.

Whenever possible, use the live URL as the primary evidence.

## 10. Build a simple placement scorecard

Ask:

- Relevant to our industry?
- Relevant to our market?
- Actual domain verified?
- Page type understood?
- Publicly accessible?
- Live URL available?
- Publication date visible?
- Support period clear?
- Fits campaign objective?

## Final thought

The value of a Chinese media placement does not come from the media name alone. It comes from the combination of relevance, page type, accessibility, verifiability and campaign fit.

PR Channel uses this approach when reviewing Chinese media placements for overseas companies.
