faq:
  - q: "How much does a PR agency in China cost?"
    a: "Retainers are usually quoted monthly and priced on the seniority and number of people assigned, not on deliverable count. Two agencies can quote very differently for the same brief because one is proposing a partner-led team and the other a junior execution team. Ask for the named team, the hours per month and what happens when the scope grows, then compare the three quotes on that basis rather than on the headline number."
  - q: "What is the difference between a PR agency and a press release distribution service in China?"
    a: "An agency sells ongoing counsel and execution — strategy, messaging, media relationships, events and reporting — usually on a monthly retainer. A distribution service sells the placement of a prepared announcement into Chinese media, priced per release, with or without verification. An agency retains the advisory layer; a distribution service assumes you already know what you want to say."
  - q: "Do I need a China-based agency or can a global agency handle it?"
    a: "A global network with a China office can be the better choice when one team must coordinate several markets, or when the account needs global brand consistency. A Chinese-origin firm is usually stronger on domestic media relationships, platform operations and the practical mechanics of publishing. Many multinationals run both: a global network for counsel and a local firm for execution."
  - q: "Can a foreign company hire a Chinese PR agency directly?"
    a: "Yes, a foreign entity can contract a China-registered agency directly. Before signing, confirm the contracting entity's registered name and unified social credit code, that the entity invoicing you is the same one you signed with, and whether the work will be subcontracted. Payment terms, currency and the tax treatment of the invoice should be settled before the scope is agreed."
  - q: "How do I verify a Chinese media placement an agency reports?"
    a: "Ask for the live URL of each published article, on the receiving media outlet's own domain, and check that the page is publicly accessible without login, that the article body matches what you approved, and that the page is still up after 30 days. A screenshot, a PDF or a link on an aggregator page is not the same evidence as an article page on the outlet's domain."

<p class="answer-lead"><strong>There is no single best PR agency in China.</strong> Pick by what the account actually needs: a global network when one team must run several markets; a Chinese-origin group when the work is domestic media and platform execution at scale; an independent firm when the work is public affairs, policy or crisis; an execution vendor when you already have the strategy and only need placement.</p>

<h2>Four businesses that all call themselves a PR agency in China</h2>
<p>Most buying mistakes in this market come from comparing quotes across these four categories as if they were the same service. They are not. The cost structure, the people, and the deliverable are different in each.</p>

<h3>1. The China office of a global network</h3>
<p>An international agency brand with a China entity, usually in Beijing and Shanghai, often also Guangzhou, Shenzhen or Hong Kong. You get a global playbook, a regional account structure and a China team that adapts both. The trade-off is that the person who wins the pitch is often not the person who runs the account, and that global process can slow down work that needs a same-week decision.</p>
<p>Choose this when the account has to stay consistent across several markets, or when headquarters procurement already has a framework agreement with the network.</p>

<h3>2. A Chinese-origin marketing and communications group</h3>
<p>Domestic groups, some listed on the Shenzhen or Shanghai exchanges, with deep relationships across Chinese media and platform operations, and the capacity to run large campaigns with large teams. They are usually stronger on execution volume, domestic platform mechanics and speed than on advising a foreign board.</p>
<p>Choose this when the objective is reach and execution inside China, or when a Chinese brand is the audience.</p>

<h3>3. The independent international firm</h3>
<p>Single-ownership firms, often with a public affairs or reputation core. The advantage is one P&amp;L, senior people staying on the account, and fewer layers between the client and the decision-maker. The trade-off is narrower geographic coverage outside their office footprint.</p>
<p>Choose this when the work is policy, regulatory positioning, crisis or board-level reputation, where senior continuity matters more than network breadth.</p>

<h3>4. The execution vendor, which is not an agency</h3>
<p>Press release distribution, media placement, media invitation and placement verification are sold as project work, priced per release or per placement. This is the right instrument when the strategy exists and the question is only how to get a specific announcement published and verified. It is the wrong instrument when nobody has decided what the message is.</p>

<h2>How to check an agency before you pay a retainer</h2>
<p>These six checks take about an afternoon and catch most of what goes wrong in this market.</p>

<h3>1. Check the legal entity, not the brand</h3>
<p>The brand you are pitched and the entity that will invoice you are sometimes different companies. Look up the registered name on the national enterprise credit system at <a href="https://www.gsxt.gov.cn/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">gsxt.gov.cn</a> and confirm the unified social credit code, the incorporation date, whether the registered business scope actually covers public relations or project planning services, and whether there are administrative penalties on record.</p>

<h3>2. Ask who is actually on the account</h3>
<p>Request the named team with titles and the share of their time committed to you. The gap between the team in the pitch and the team after signing is the single most common complaint about agency retainers in China, and it is cheaper to test before the contract than after.</p>

<h3>3. Insist that case studies are live links</h3>
<p>A case study should resolve to an article page on the receiving outlet's own domain. A screenshot, a PDF deck or a link to an aggregator page is not the same evidence. Open three of them at random and check the article body against what the agency says it achieved.</p>

<h3>4. Confirm the contracting and invoicing entity is the same</h3>
<p>If the contract is signed with one entity and the invoice comes from another, you have no clean route to a remedy. This check costs nothing and is skipped surprisingly often.</p>

<h3>5. Ask directly whether the work will be subcontracted</h3>
<p>Large firms routinely pass execution to smaller local suppliers. That is not automatically a problem — it is often how the work gets done — but you should know it, know who the subcontractor is, and know which party is accountable for the result.</p>

<h3>6. Define "published" before you sign</h3>
<p>Agree what counts as a completed deliverable: a live article on a specified outlet's domain, publicly accessible, still live after 30 days. Without that definition, an agency can report success on the basis of syndicated copies or aggregator listings that carry no editorial weight. The verification method is set out in <a href="/how-we-evaluate-chinese-media-placements/">how we evaluate Chinese media placements</a>.</p>

<h2>When a PR agency is the wrong purchase</h2>
<p>An agency retainer buys counsel and continuity. If what you actually need is one announcement published in Chinese media and evidence that it was published, a monthly retainer is an expensive way to buy it, and the agency will usually buy the placement from a distribution supplier anyway.</p>
